Why Buddhism Is True

Notes on Robert Wright (2017) · Lukas Koebis · November 2026

These are my notes on Robert Wright's Why Buddhism Is True, which I read after my stay at Wat Pa Tam Wua. They're mainly for me, to remember the book. My own thoughts come first in each section, then the passages I highlighted. At the end I checked which of the studies he cites still hold up.

The short version

Natural selection designed a brain with an objective function — getting genes into the next generation — that doesn't optimise for good and happy lives or for seeing the truth. The Buddha described the symptoms (craving that never satisfies, a self that isn't really in charge) about 2,400 years before Darwin explained the cause, and modern psychology now lands in a similar place. Meditation is Buddhism's tool for seeing through it.

Shortest version of my answer to the question of why Buddhism is true, it's this: Because we are animals created by natural selection. Natural selection built into our brains the tendencies that early Buddhist thinkers did a pretty amazing job of sizing up, given the meager scientific resources at their disposal. Now, in light of the modern understanding of natural selection and the modern understanding of the human brain that natural selection produced, we can provide a new kind of defense of this sizing up.

A brain designed by natural selection

The brain was designed by natural selection, and the objective function was to get genes into the next generation. That got us to a really good place — we have an intelligent brain — but a lot of what goes on in it doesn't serve us if the goal is a fulfilling, happy life. Evolution left many artefacts that don't fit our lives any more.

So if you ask the question "What kinds of perceptions and thoughts and feelings guide us through life each day?" the answer, at the most basic level, isn't "The kinds of thoughts and feelings and perceptions that give us an accurate picture of reality." No, at the most basic level the answer is "The kinds of thoughts and feelings and perceptions that helped our ancestors get genes into the next generation." Whether those thoughts and feelings and perceptions give us a true view of reality is, strictly speaking, beside the point.

Natural selection designed our feelings in a particular environment — an environment with no junk food, an environment in which the sweetest thing available was fruit. So a sweet tooth served us well … But in a modern environment, which features the achievement of culinary science known as "empty calories," these feelings become "false."

Our feelings weren't designed to depict reality accurately even in our "natural" environment. … The fact that we're not living in a "natural" environment makes our feelings even less reliable guides to reality.

Craving

Tanha (craving, desire) was also designed by evolution to keep the body alive: always craving food, sugar, shelter. Now that we have safety, security, and enough food, it doesn't lead to much happiness, and Buddhism questions it.

Tanha isn't only about wanting good things. It's also about avoiding bad ones — loud noise, pain, discomfort. Wright makes the same point with the "three poisons" usually translated as greed, hatred, and delusion: greed is really attraction to anything, hatred is aversion to anything, and together they produce the delusion.

The first time you try something new, like a doughnut, you get a lot of dopamine after eating it. Later your brain already knows what it's like, so the dopamine comes before you eat it — the brain is trying to get you to eat it — and when you actually do, it falls flat. That's useful for quitting sugar and similar habits: the pull is the anticipation, and the thing itself under-delivers.

The pre-bite dopamine blast you're now getting is the promise of more bliss, and the post-bite drop in dopamine is, in a way, the breaking of the promise — or, at least, it's a kind of biochemical acknowledgment that there was some overpromising.

Though the Buddha is famous for asserting that life is pervaded by suffering, some scholars say that's an incomplete rendering of his message and that the word translated as "suffering," dukkha, could, for some purposes, be translated as "unsatisfactoriness."

"The cost of survival of the lineage may be a lifetime of discomfort."

Aaron Beck, founder of cognitive-behavioural therapy

Not-self

The brain has almost independent networks — Wright calls them modules, though that's more a metaphor than established science — each doing its own thing, all firing and trying to get the attention of consciousness. Consciousness is the part that brings it together and makes it feel like one coherent train of thought, but often that's an illusion.

That's the illusion of self: we feel in control, acting on what our self wants. In practice, things pop into our heads for reasons we can't control, and that steers what happens next. Instead of you thinking the thoughts, it's more like the thoughts are trying to capture you.

When the mind "wanders" from one module to another, what's actually happening is that the second module has acquired enough strength to wrestle control of your consciousness away from the first module.

Whereas you might have thought all your life that you're thinking thoughts — the thing you think of as 'you' is thinking thoughts — it's closer to being the case that the thoughts try to capture you.

One theory in the book is that every thought comes with an emotion, and the emotion is what drives it. Everything you think carries a slight positive or negative valuation.

It's feelings that "decide" which module will be in charge for the time being, and it's modules that then decide what you'll actually do during that time.

So two of the properties commonly associated with a self — control and persistence through time — are found to be absent, not evident in any of the five components that seem to constitute human beings.

"Look, if there's part of you that isn't under your control and therefore makes you suffer, then do yourself a favor and quit identifying with it!"

Having a self that always has an explanation was useful in evolution. Someone who confidently gives reasons for what they did makes a better ally than someone who says "I have no idea why I did this" — even when the reasons are made up. Wright's example is a split-brain patient whose right hemisphere was told to walk; when asked where he was going, his left hemisphere made up an answer on the spot.

He seems like a guy who is in charge of himself, who doesn't go around doing things for no good reason. Compare him with a guy who offers a more truthful account: "I don't really know why I got up or where I'm going." … If those two guys were your neighbors in a hunter-gatherer village, which one would you want to go hunting with?

People think they're above average on almost everything — fairness, being a good person. So there are two illusions: first, that you're in control, and second, that you're a good person, better than others, who deserves more. It comes back to natural selection: if you want to pass on your genes, it helps to believe that you and your genes are better. That's deeply programmed into us.

So, all told, we're under at least two kinds of illusions. One is about the nature of the conscious self, which we see as more in control of things than it actually is. The other illusion is about exactly what kind of people we are — namely, capable and upstanding. You might call these two misconceptions the illusion about our selves and the illusion about ourselves. They work in synergy.

"I consider myself an average man except for the fact that I consider myself an average man."

Montaigne

When we recount an experience to someone, the act of recounting it changes the memory of it. So if we reshape the story a bit each time — omitting inconvenient facts, exaggerating convenient ones — we can, over time, transform our actual belief about what happened.

Constant judgment, and emptiness

Natural selection wants you to judge everything: is it good for me or bad for me? So the brain is constantly judging. Buddhism — maybe the better way to live — teaches you to stop. Things aren't good or bad; you observe them as they are. That gets you closer to the truth than the good/bad bias does.

Natural selection "wants" you to experience things as either good or bad. The Buddha believed that the less you judge things — including the contents of your mind — the more clearly you'll see them, and the less deluded you'll be.

"We do not just see 'a house': we see 'a handsome house,' 'an ugly house,' or 'a pretentious house.'"

Robert Zajonc, 1980

No self, no boundary

If the self doesn't exist, there's no real boundary between you and the outside world. Meditating on that leads either to emptiness (Buddhism) or to the idea that everything is connected, one big soul (Hinduism). So not-self and emptiness are closely linked. It also ties back to judging: judgment makes it very clear that something is outside you and either good or bad for you. Stop judging and you may feel not-self and emptiness more clearly.

From a Darwinian perspective, tanha was engineered into us so that we would take care of ourselves — which is to say, so that each of us would take care of the vehicle that contains our genes. And that vehicle stops at the skin, at the bounds of the body.

Maybe the deepest meditative experiences had by Buddhists and the deepest meditative experiences had by Hindus in the Advaita Vedanta tradition are basically the same experience. There is a sense of dissolution of the bounds of self and an ensuing sense of continuity with the world out there. … The core experiences remain the same, but the doctrinal articulation varies.

The view from nowhere

Wright compares this with Einstein. In everyday physics you do everything from your own point of view, and that works fine for real life. But to understand how the universe actually works, you can't have a privileged reference point — that's what relativity does. Enlightenment might be similar: a view from nowhere, with no bias from your position or anyone else's.

The view from nowhere may be the pithiest way of describing what Buddhist enlightenment would be like: the view that carries none of my selfish biases, or yours, and that in a certain sense isn't even a particularly human perspective. … This, truly, would be a view that defied natural selection's authority, because natural selection is all about specific perspectives.

You can't think your way there

A lot of this doesn't make much sense when you reason about it; it has to be experienced. That's the difference between knowledge and wisdom: wisdom has to be lived.

"To understand not-self, you have to meditate," he advised. If you try to grasp the doctrine through "intellectualizing" alone, "your head will explode."

"Ultimately, happiness comes down to choosing between the discomfort of becoming aware of your mental afflictions and the discomfort of being ruled by them."

Yongey Mingyur Rinpoche

The more you can watch your mind — desires, emotions — instead of being pushed around by it, the more choice you get. You can choose which feelings to follow, and focus on the good ones.

Experiencing your feelings with care and clarity, rather than following them reflexively and uncritically, lets you choose which ones to follow — like, say, joy, delight, and love.

The practical tools in the book:

  • RAIN: Recognise the feeling, Accept it, Investigate how it shows up in the body, then Non-identification.
  • Jealousy and other strong feelings: don't resist them; watch them as they start, so the module never takes over.
  • Aversion: Ajahn Chah, disturbed by a village festival while meditating — "The sound is just the sound. It's me who is going out to annoy it."
  • Daily practice: Wright sits with anger, anxiety, or the urge to procrastinate until it weakens. The morning sits are what make him remember that's an option.

"Don't try to use what you learn from Buddhism to be a better Buddhist; use it to be a better whatever-you-already-are."

The Dalai Lama

Do the studies hold up?

A lot of psychology studies have never been replicated, so I looked into the research Wright cites (November 2026). The core argument holds up; the weakest studies are side examples that the thesis doesn't need.

ClaimVerdict
Dopamine fires before an expected reward, not at it (the doughnut)Solid
Schultz et al. 1997 in monkeys, replicated in many labs and in humans. Two refinements: dopamine signals wanting and prediction error rather than pleasure (Berridge), and a fully expected reward produces no change at the bite — there's only a dip if it's worse than expected.
We overrate everything about ourselves, especially our moralsSolid
The better-than-average effect is one of the most replicated findings in psychology. Some of it is a statistical artefact (skewed distributions, vague traits), but the moral version is the strongest (Tappin & McKay 2017).
Retelling a story changes the memorySolid
Misinformation effect (Loftus) and "saying is believing" (Higgins; Echterhoff), replicated many times.
Every perception carries a feelingMostly solid
Fast automatic evaluation (Fazio, Bargh) replicates well. The subliminal-priming versions are shaky.
Hedonic treadmillPartly
The famous lottery study (Brickman 1978) had about 22 winners. A large, preregistered Swedish study (Lindqvist et al. 2020) found life-satisfaction gains lasting more than a decade; effects on day-to-day happiness were smaller. Adaptation is real but incomplete.
The conscious self isn't in charge (Libet)Contested
Libet's "readiness potential" may just be accumulated noise, not a hidden decision (Schurger et al. 2012; summary). Flicking a finger also says little about real decisions. The weaker claim — consciousness has less control than it feels like — survives on other evidence.
Split-brain confabulation (the soda)Real, tiny sample
Gazzaniga's findings come from a handful of patients, and Pinto et al. 2017 challenged the "two minds" reading. The healthy-person version, choice blindness (Johansson & Hall 2005), has replicated.
East and West both self-enhanceDisputed
Heine's meta-analyses say East Asians self-enhance much less; Sedikides says they do it on collectivist traits instead. Wright takes one side.
The mind is made of modulesFramework, not finding
"Massive modularity" is heavily debated, including by Fodor, who coined the idea of modules. It's a useful metaphor, not an established fact.
Meditators quiet the default mode network; meditation helpsModest
The default mode network is well established; Brewer et al. 2011 on meditators was small. A large meta-analysis (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA) found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression, and pain, with small effects, often no better than exercise or therapy.
Rejection uses the same brain areas as physical painOverstated
Eisenberger 2003 was small. Woo et al. 2014 showed that the two brain patterns can be told apart; overlapping areas don't mean the same process.
Tylenol dulls social painUnconfirmed
DeWall et al. 2010 was small; I couldn't find a strong independent replication, and one thesis found nothing.
Men near women care more about wealthProbably false
The "mating priming" studies failed to replicate, and a meta-analysis found strong signs of publication bias (Shanks et al. 2015).
Self-control is a muscleFailed
Ego depletion: a 23-lab replication with about 2,100 people (Hagger et al. 2016) and a second multi-lab test (Vohs et al. 2021) both found roughly zero effect.
