Wat Pa Tam Wua

Lukas Koebis · September 2026

I spent 8 days at Wat Pa Tam Wua, a Buddhist monastery in northern Thailand (near Mae Hong Son). Apart from a 30-day Sam Harris meditation course through an app that I did 10 years prior, I had no meditation experience. I've had an amazing experience and never felt as much inner peace and clarity before. It felt like my brain went back to its base state after being polluted by phone notifications, emails, podcasts, YouTube videos, … for years. This is very similar to what the book The Little Book of Clarity by Jamie Smart summarises as "Clarity = Capacity − Contamination". I would highly recommend everyone to attend this monastery or find a similar one to experience their mind in its uncontaminated state.

Monastery life

My brother currently lives in Chiang Mai, so after spending a few weeks with him there we took the 5-hour drive via Route 1095 and almost 1000 curves (motion sickness pills recommended!) to Wat Pa Tam Wua. You don't need to register in advance. Every day you can check in between 6am and 4pm and stay 2 to 10 nights. Some people I met there have been staying for months. The monastery is free to attend and lives off donations. They give you white clothes to wear, and there's a small shop onsite for things like mosquito repellent, shower gel, etc.

Wat Pa Tam Wua was established by Ajahn Luongta Saiyut. He spent decades as an ascetic wandering forest monk in solitary meditation inside deep mountain caves and wilderness throughout Thailand. This time inspired him to set up Wat Pa Tam Wua as a peaceful, nature-immersed refuge for both Thais and foreigners to learn meditation. He didn't run any of the Dharma talks while I was there but made himself available for questions. He seemed like a very happy and chill guy — if I've ever met someone enlightened then it was him.

The monks follow 277 rules but guests just have to follow the Eight Precepts:

  1. abstinence from taking life
  2. abstinence from taking what is not given
  3. abstinence from unchastity
  4. abstinence from false speech
  5. abstinence from intoxicants which cause a careless frame of mind
  6. abstinence from taking food at the wrong time
  7. abstinence from dancing, music, visiting shows, flowers, make-up, the wearing of ornaments and decorations
  8. abstinence from a luxurious or high sleeping place

365 days a year the monastery follows the same schedule:

05:00 Morning meditation by yourself
06:30 Rice offering to the monks
07:00 Breakfast
08:00 Morning Dharma talk and meditation class
10:30 Food offering to the monks
11:00 Lunch
12:50 Afternoon Dharma talk and meditation class
16:00 Cleaning the area and helping in the monastery
18:00 Evening chanting, meditation

It took a few days to get into the rhythm. After 2 days I thought I should cut my trip short but I'm very glad I stayed. Day 5–7 were the best by far in terms of clarity.

You wake up early in the most beautiful and surreal landscape. It feels like some Avatar world between two mountains and a small river running through the monastery.

The day starts with solo meditation but most people slept and just got up for the 6:30 rice offerings. Monks are not allowed to cook, store food overnight, or farm for themselves. Rice offerings are not considered charity but a symbiotic relationship between monks and lay people:

  • Monks provide spiritual guidance, a living example of mindfulness and virtue.
  • Lay people provide physical nourishment.
  • Giving alms allows laypeople to practice generosity, let go of greed/attachment, and earn positive spiritual merit.

Note that you have all your food before 12:00. There's a cafe onsite if you get hungry later. After the first two days I followed the rule of not having food after 12:00. Some people also fasted for the whole time they were there. Fasting is something I'd like to try sometime as well.

I stayed in a shared room on a wooden bed. Sleep was actually the hardest part — especially if you sleep on the side it's very tough on such a hard surface. There are single rooms available and I could've gotten one but I didn't want to bother with moving.

The 16:00 work session was usually also a highlight of the day. You're not supposed to do any sports so some physical work feels refreshing after many hours of meditation. I started by raking leaves, later helped set up a new fruit garden, and towards the end helped run the introduction sessions for new arrivals.

Meditation

The morning and afternoon sessions have the same structure: short Dharma talk, 60min walking meditation, 40min sitting meditation, 20min lying-down meditation. For someone who is new to meditation this is a great setup. Sitting for 2h wouldn't really be possible for most beginners.

There are two types of meditation: Samatha (concentration) and Vipassana (insight).

Walking meditation takes a while to get used to and it trains you in mindfulness. You walk about 1km in 1 hour barefoot through the monastery and focus on everything you feel: the wet floor under your feet, the sun touching your skin, the wind in your face, … Your eyes stay open but you try not to get distracted by seeing anything. The goal is to reach a state where it feels like your body is some kind of robot and you're just there observing it (knower state). This is true Vipassana meditation — you try to see how experience actually works in real time and gain insight into impermanence (see Buddhism section). In the last few days I think I've felt this for a few seconds at a time but it needs a lot more practice.

From Walk to be the Knower (monastery site · local copy): "The mind is just the watcher. The body moves and the mind watches. The body ceases to be Ours. This body moves but it is not us moving. True mindfulness arises. The mind awakens and sees that the physical form that is moving is not us."

"Knower" vs "Thinker" mind:

  • can't be both at the same time
  • observe the breath and then observe the mind "knowing" the breath to get to the Knower's state
  • get to a point when you understand that the Knower knows the breath and not you (selflessness)
  • you can't intend for the Knower to arise

During the sitting and lying-down meditation you just focus on your breath — Samatha meditation. I can't comfortably sit on the floor for 40min so I usually sat on a chair. I'll try to get better at sitting on the floor over the next months. Every meditation book recommends this. But even with chair sitting meditation I got to quite interesting states of mind (keep reading).

Think of Samatha like sharpening a pencil, and Vipassana like using that sharp pencil to write clearly. Samatha stills the turbulent mind so it isn't constantly jumping around. Vipassana uses that clear, concentrated mind to observe life without getting distracted by reactions or mental noise.

In the evening we chanted in Pali (Buddha's language), Thai, and English, and did another 30min of sitting meditation. The evening sessions were my favourite. The chanting is quite melodic and since you repeat the same phrases every day you get really into it after a few days. Then you start the meditation just before it gets dark and when you open your eyes again it's dark outside. Occasionally it would also start raining during this time. The vibes were 10/10. Also it's over 6h after the last meal so your mind is very clear.

Chanting book: monastery site · local copy

I think on my last two nights I got into some kind of absorption that felt like what people describe as a Jhana state — I'm not confident enough in the terminology to claim it was the real thing. Every breath in felt like bright light reaching every part of my body. You're grateful for each breath and it gives you immense joy to just exist. Each experience lasted roughly 5–10min. It's quite an interesting state because you don't have to actively focus on your breath anymore, you have some slight control of your mind and you can control your breath and have some thoughts (e.g. direct your thoughts to visuals).

In terms of technique, what worked for me: take 5–10 deep breaths in and follow your breath with your mind (i.e. the air comes in through your nose and goes deep inside your body and you follow that movement with your mind). Then stop controlling the breath and just observe the natural breath at a single point in the nose (usually the breath in and breath out hit a slightly different point for me but I tried to find a single point that is hit by both in and out breath). Whenever you get distracted by other thoughts, just notice them, and go back to the breath. When I meditate now this happens a lot. A few days into the monastery experience I basically never got distracted. After 5–10min of deep focus it feels like you're almost entering some new state where you're "deeper" in your mind. Once you get there I felt like you don't even have to focus on the breath anymore and you just stay there. At that point I started to focus on visuals. You see pitch black but occasionally you might see a pattern or point and then try to hold on to it. I found this part quite difficult but doing this I reached this state twice during lie-down meditation.

Another interesting experience I had during sitting meditation was that it felt like my body was deforming. I usually feel my hands resting on my legs and after a while it felt like the distance between head and legs would increase and decrease. Then after a while it felt like my body and mind became a single point. This was quite scary and I opened my eyes and ended the meditation. I haven't been able to get to this point again but it would be interesting to see what happens after. Maybe this gets you closer to really understanding no-self (see Buddhism section).

Note that this wasn't a strict silent Vipassana retreat. Some people had "silence" labels and didn't talk while they were there, others were browsing TikTok in between meditations and had instant noodles at the cafe for dinner. I turned off my phone while I was there, read many books, took notes, and had a couple conversations with people there but mainly stuck to myself. Note that in strict Vipassana retreats you're not allowed to talk, read and write — this sounds very intense, not sure I'd be able to do that.

The monastery published Walk to be the Knower (monastery site · local copy) which describes all this in more detail.

One monk described how you spend a lot of time focusing on your body (eating food, brushing your teeth, going to the gym, …) but most people spend very little time focusing on their mind. Meditation is like going to the gym for the mind. Even if you just do it for a few minutes each morning or evening, it's worth it.

Buddhism

This was a Theravada Buddhist monastery (mainly Southeast Asia). There are other forms (Mahayana in Northeast Asia, …). All my learnings came from a one-day course in Chiang Mai and from my reading at the monastery.

I mostly engaged with Buddhism as a philosophy / practice rather than a religion — though in Thai culture (and many others) it clearly functions as one (temples, …).

The Buddha (the enlightened one) lived around the 5th–6th century BCE. The core of the belief system are the Four Noble Truths:

  • Dukkha: our existence is bound up with suffering
  • Samudaya: the cause of suffering is desire
  • Nirodha: solution is extinction of suffering
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path is the way to end suffering

The Noble Eightfold Path:

  • Right view, thought (Wisdom, Paññā)
  • Right speech, action, livelihood (Morality, Sīla)
  • Right effort, mindfulness, concentration (Concentration, Samādhi)

To gain wisdom you need to contemplate the three Characteristics of Existence:

  • impermanence (anicca)
  • unsatisfactoriness (dukkha)
  • non-self / selflessness (anattā)

The mental defilements which hinder spiritual development are:

  • attachment to pleasures of the 5 senses
  • ill-will or hatred towards others
  • sloth and torpor (inaction to make wholesome action)
  • restlessness, anxiety, worry
  • sceptical doubt (inclination to reject ideas without reasoning)

The only way beyond doubt: understand them as impermanent, conditioned mental states.

There's ordinary wisdom (expressed in words, understood with the ordinary mind) vs wisdom of knowing things at the deepest level (beyond words/concepts) — that has to be discovered / experienced by yourself.

From Hesse's Siddhartha: "Wisdom cannot be passed on. Wisdom which a wise man tries to pass on to someone always sounds like foolishness."

"Knowledge can be conveyed, but not wisdom. It can be found, it can be lived […] but it cannot be expressed in words and taught."

I asked some questions to the Abbot but didn't get any concrete answers. It's all about practicing more / years. Your body and mind are the best teacher.

I've asked quite a few questions after the Dharma talks but none of the answers were very satisfying. I guess the key is just that your own mind is the source of truth and you need to figure things out yourself.

Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipatthana)

  • mindfulness of the body
  • mindfulness of feelings
  • mindfulness of the mind
  • mindfulness of mental qualities (dhamma)

Usually people mainly focus on mindfulness of the body (focus on breath). This has been around even before the Buddha. The Buddha tried this for many years but had a breakthrough when focusing on mindfulness of the mind. The monk who gave the Dharma talks shared a similar story from his own practice. Apparently people who are more logical can make more progress by focusing on mindfulness of the mind.

Takeaways

The mind is the only real source of truth available to us. Even physics and what we call reality is all just perceived through the mind. Everyone should spend some time going deeper in understanding what your mind is like.

I really like the Buddhist philosophy and especially that the Buddha is saying that the only teacher is your own body and mind and that everyone needs to go through the path themselves. I think a lot of technical / rational people could get behind this belief and I'm sure it'll become a more popular philosophy in the West.

I don't think I can get behind: rebirth, kamma, world view, … and some other parts of the Dhamma. What (I think) I'd miss adopting a Buddhist life fully? Procreation, innovation, music, beauty, art.

I've started reading some more books about neuroscience and the scientific side of what we know about the mind (still not much). One part I found interesting is that the "mind" basically consists of a lot of independent parts — Robert Wright's Why Buddhism Is True goes into this via evolutionary psychology: modular minds, the illusion of a unified self, and how mindfulness can weaken the modules that generate craving and aversion.

Stoicism vs Buddhism, briefly:

  • Managing emotions: Both traditions advise against reacting impulsively to painful events. A Stoic re-evaluates their judgment ("This situation isn't inherently bad; my perception makes it so"), whereas a Buddhist observes the feeling mindfully without identifying with it, letting it pass like a cloud.
  • Acceptance of impermanence: Stoics practice Memento Mori (remembering death) to appreciate the present and build resilience against loss. Buddhists meditate on anicca to dissolve attachment to temporary phenomena altogether.

After spending a week at the monastery I flew to Shanghai to explore China with some friends. I'm very impressed by China (requires another post) and various parts here seem to be the opposite of Buddhism and monastery life (bath houses for £30 for 24h with unlimited food, games, …; everything is so cheap on Taobao — consumerism/capitalism at its peak). While travelling it's also difficult to keep a more mindful/quiet routine alive. I'll try to make some more changes to my life based on my learnings when I'm back in London. It would be great to find a path to at least retain some of the clarity / peace of mind:

  • limit phone / laptop time
  • be more thoughtful about what content to take in (podcasts, YT, news, …)
  • walking meditation + sitting meditation
  • yoga, sports, hot/cold therapy
  • rethink food setup (maybe don't eat during certain times, maybe implement regular fasts, …)
  • re-read monastery journal entries
  • reading and writing
  • having a project / topic you deeply care about and spend a few hours every day on it
  • control sensual desires more
  • practice loving kindness more
  • helping people, be more involved in some community
