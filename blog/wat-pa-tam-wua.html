Wat Pa Tam Wua

Lukas Koebis · September 2026

I spent 8 days at Wat Pa Tam Wua, a Buddhist monastery in northern Thailand (near Mae Hong Son). Apart from a 30-day Sam Harris meditation course through an app that I did 10 years prior, I had no meditation experience. I've had an amazing experience and never felt as much inner peace and clarity before. It felt like my brain went back to its base state after being polluted by phone notifications, emails, podcasts, YouTube videos, … for years.

This is very similar to what Jamie Smart summarises in The Little Book of Clarity as "Clarity = Capacity − Contamination". I would highly recommend everyone to attend this monastery or find a similar one to experience their mind in its uncontaminated state.

Monastery life

My brother currently lives in Chiang Mai, so after spending a few weeks with him there we took the 5-hour drive via Route 1095 and almost 1000 curves (motion sickness pills recommended!) to Wat Pa Tam Wua. You don't need to register in advance. Every day you can check in between 6am and 4pm and stay 2 to 10 nights. Some people I met there have been staying for months. The monastery is free to attend and lives off donations. They give you white clothes to wear, and there's a small shop onsite for things like mosquito repellent, shower gel, etc.

Wat Pa Tam Wua was established by Ajahn Luongta Saiyut. He spent decades as an ascetic wandering forest monk in solitary meditation inside deep mountain caves and wilderness throughout Thailand. This time inspired him to set up Wat Pa Tam Wua as a peaceful, nature-immersed refuge for both Thais and foreigners to learn meditation. He didn't run any of the Dharma talks while I was there but made himself available for questions. He seemed like a very happy and chill guy — if I've ever met someone enlightened then it was him.

The monks follow 277 rules but guests just have to follow the Eight Precepts:

  1. abstinence from taking life
  2. abstinence from taking what is not given
  3. abstinence from unchastity
  4. abstinence from false speech
  5. abstinence from intoxicants which cause a careless frame of mind
  6. abstinence from taking food at the wrong time
  7. abstinence from dancing, music, visiting shows, flowers, make-up, the wearing of ornaments and decorations
  8. abstinence from a luxurious or high sleeping place

365 days a year the monastery follows the same schedule:

05:00 Morning meditation by yourself
06:30 Rice offering to the monks
07:00 Breakfast
08:00 Morning Dharma talk and meditation class
10:30 Food offering to the monks
11:00 Lunch
12:50 Afternoon Dharma talk and meditation class
16:00 Cleaning the area and helping in the monastery
18:00 Evening chanting, meditation

It took a few days to get into the rhythm. After 2 days I thought I should cut my trip short but I'm very glad I stayed. Day 5–7 were the best by far in terms of clarity.

You wake up early in the most beautiful and surreal landscape. It feels like some Avatar world between two mountains and a small river running through the monastery.

The day starts with solo meditation but most people slept and just got up for the 6:30 rice offerings. Monks are not allowed to cook, store food overnight, or farm for themselves. Rice offerings are not considered charity but a symbiotic relationship between monks and lay people:

  • Monks provide spiritual guidance, a living example of mindfulness and virtue.
  • Lay people provide physical nourishment.
  • Giving alms allows laypeople to practice generosity, let go of greed/attachment, and earn positive spiritual merit.

Note that you have all your food before 12:00. There's a cafe onsite if you get hungry later. After the first two days I followed the rule of not having food after 12:00. Some people also fasted for the whole time they were there. Fasting is something I'd like to try sometime as well.

I stayed in a shared room on a wooden bed. Sleep was actually the hardest part — especially if you sleep on the side it's very tough on such a hard surface. There are single rooms available and I could've gotten one but I didn't want to bother with moving.

The 16:00 work session was usually also a highlight of the day. You're not supposed to do any sports so some physical work feels refreshing after many hours of meditation. I started by raking leaves, later helped set up a new fruit garden, and towards the end helped run the introduction sessions for new arrivals.

Meditation

The morning and afternoon sessions have the same structure: short Dharma talk, 60min walking meditation, 40min sitting meditation, 20min lying-down meditation. For someone who is new to meditation this is a great setup. Sitting for 2h wouldn't really be possible for most beginners.

There are two types of meditation: Samatha (concentration) and Vipassana (insight). Think of Samatha like sharpening a pencil, and Vipassana like using that sharp pencil to write clearly. Samatha stills the turbulent mind so it isn't constantly jumping around. Vipassana uses that clear, concentrated mind to observe life without getting distracted by reactions or mental noise.

Walking meditation takes a while to get used to. You walk about 1km in 1 hour barefoot through the monastery and focus on everything you feel: the wet floor under your feet, the sun touching your skin, the wind in your face, … Eyes stay open, but you try not to get distracted by seeing anything. The goal is a state where the body feels like some kind of robot and you're just there observing it — the "knower" state. You can't be knower and thinker at the same time. One way in is to watch the breath, then watch the mind knowing the breath, until it clicks that the knower knows the breath — not "you". You also can't intend for the knower to arise. In the last few days I think I've felt this for a few seconds at a time, but it needs a lot more practice.

From Walk to be the Knower (monastery site · local copy): "The mind is just the watcher. The body moves and the mind watches. The body ceases to be Ours. This body moves but it is not us moving. True mindfulness arises. The mind awakens and sees that the physical form that is moving is not us."

During sitting and lying-down meditation you just focus on the breath — Samatha. I can't comfortably sit on the floor for 40min so I usually sat on a chair. I'll try to get better at floor sitting over the next months (every meditation book recommends it). Even with a chair I got to some quite interesting states of mind.

In the evening we chanted in Pali (the Buddha's language), Thai, and English, then did another 30min of sitting meditation. The evening sessions were my favourite. The chanting is quite melodic, and since you repeat the same phrases every day you get really into it after a few days. You start the meditation just before it gets dark, and when you open your eyes again it's dark outside. Occasionally it would also start raining. The vibes were 10/10. Also it's over 6h after the last meal, so your mind is very clear.

Chanting book: monastery site · local copy

I think on my last two nights I got into some kind of absorption that felt like what people describe as a Jhana state — I'm not confident enough in the terminology to claim it was the real thing. Every breath in felt like bright light reaching every part of my body. You're grateful for each breath and it gives you immense joy to just exist. Each experience lasted roughly 5–10min. You don't have to actively focus on the breath anymore; you have some slight control of your mind, can steer the breath a bit, and can still have thoughts (e.g. direct them toward visuals).

In terms of technique, what worked for me: take 5–10 deep breaths and follow the breath with your mind — the air comes in through the nose, goes deep into the body, and you track that movement. Then stop controlling it and just observe the natural breath at a single point on the nose. (In and out usually hit slightly different points for me; I tried to find one spot that both pass through.) Whenever other thoughts show up, notice them and go back to the breath. When I meditate now this happens a lot. A few days into the monastery I basically never got distracted. After 5–10min of deep focus it feels like you're entering some deeper state — and once you're there, you don't even have to focus on the breath anymore, you just stay. At that point I started focusing on visuals: pitch black, then occasionally a pattern or point you'd try to hold onto. I found that part quite difficult, but that's how I reached this state twice during lie-down meditation.

Another interesting experience during sitting: it felt like my body was deforming. I usually feel my hands resting on my legs, and after a while the distance between head and legs seemed to increase and decrease. Then it felt like body and mind collapsed into a single point. That was quite scary — I opened my eyes and ended the meditation. I haven't gotten back there, but it would be interesting to see what happens next. Maybe that's closer to really understanding no-self (see Buddhism section).

This wasn't a strict silent Vipassana retreat. Some people had "silence" labels and didn't talk; others were browsing TikTok between sessions and eating instant noodles at the cafe for dinner. I turned my phone off, read a lot, took notes, had a couple of conversations, but mainly stuck to myself. Strict Vipassana retreats ban talking, reading, and writing — sounds very intense. Not sure I'd be able to do that.

One monk put it well: you spend a lot of time focusing on your body (eating, brushing your teeth, going to the gym, …) but most people spend very little time focusing on their mind. Meditation is like going to the gym for the mind. Even a few minutes each morning or evening is worth it.

Buddhism

This was a Theravada Buddhist monastery (mainly Southeast Asia). There are other forms (Mahayana in Northeast Asia, …). All my learnings came from a one-day course in Chiang Mai and from reading at the monastery.

I mostly engaged with Buddhism as a philosophy / practice rather than a religion — though in Thai culture (and many others) it clearly functions as one (temples, …).

The Buddha (the enlightened one) lived around the 5th–6th century BC. The core of the belief system are the Four Noble Truths:

  • Dukkha: our existence is bound up with suffering
  • Samudaya: the cause of suffering is desire
  • Nirodha: solution is extinction of suffering
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path is the way to end suffering

The Noble Eightfold Path:

  • Right view, thought (Wisdom, Paññā)
  • Right speech, action, livelihood (Morality, Sīla)
  • Right effort, mindfulness, concentration (Concentration, Samādhi)

To gain wisdom you need to contemplate the three Characteristics of Existence:

  • impermanence (anicca)
  • unsatisfactoriness (dukkha)
  • non-self / selflessness (anattā)

There's ordinary wisdom (expressed in words, understood with the ordinary mind) vs wisdom of knowing things at the deepest level (beyond words/concepts) — that has to be discovered / experienced by yourself.

From Hesse's Siddhartha: "Wisdom cannot be passed on. Wisdom which a wise man tries to pass on to someone always sounds like foolishness."

"Knowledge can be conveyed, but not wisdom. It can be found, it can be lived […] but it cannot be expressed in words and taught."

I asked quite a few questions after the Dharma talks and to the Abbot. None of the answers were very concrete — more practice, more years; your body and mind are the teacher. Annoying if you want explanations, but that is kind of the point. The monk who gave the talks kept coming back to a story along these lines: a student is asked whether he believes the Buddha, says no, and the Buddha basically says good — you shouldn't believe me, go investigate yourself. I couldn't find that exact Q&A in the suttas; the closest official version is the Kalama Sutta, where the Buddha tells people not to take teachings on authority (including "the monk is our teacher") and to know for themselves. Either way, that was the vibe of every answer I got.

Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipatthana)

  • mindfulness of the body
  • mindfulness of feelings
  • mindfulness of the mind
  • mindfulness of mental qualities (dhamma)

Usually people mainly focus on mindfulness of the body (the breath). That practice existed even before the Buddha. He tried it for many years and had a breakthrough when he started focusing on mindfulness of the mind. The monk who gave the Dharma talks shared a similar story from his own practice — apparently more logical people can make more progress that way.

Takeaways

The mind is the only real source of truth available to us. Even physics and what we call reality is all just perceived through the mind. Everyone should spend some time going deeper into what their mind is actually like.

I really like the Buddhist philosophy — especially that the only teacher is your own body and mind, and everyone has to walk the path themselves. A lot of technical / rational people could get behind that, and I wouldn't be surprised if it keeps getting more popular in the West.

I don't think I can get behind: rebirth, kamma, world view, … and some other parts of the Dhamma. What (I think) I'd miss adopting a Buddhist life fully? Procreation, innovation, music, beauty, art.

I've started reading more on the scientific side of the mind (still early). Robert Wright's Why Buddhism Is True is the one I'm in now: evolutionary psychology meets meditation. The basic claim that clicked for me is that the "self" isn't a CEO running the show — it's more like a bunch of modules competing (craving, status, fear, …), and the feeling of a unified "I" is something the brain stitches together after the fact. Mindfulness is useful here because you can watch a craving module fire without having to obey it. That lines up uncannily well with what the monastery was pointing at with knower vs thinker and no-self, just in a different vocabulary.

Stoicism vs Buddhism

  • Managing emotions: Both say don't react impulsively to painful events. A Stoic re-evaluates the judgment ("this situation isn't inherently bad; my perception makes it so"). A Buddhist watches the feeling without identifying with it and lets it pass like a cloud.
  • Impermanence: Stoics practice Memento Mori to appreciate the present and build resilience against loss. Buddhists meditate on anicca to dissolve attachment to temporary phenomena altogether.

After the monastery I flew to Shanghai with some friends. I'm very impressed by China (another post). Being there is also kind of the opposite pole — e.g. a 24h bath house for £30 with unlimited food and games, Taobao-level consumerism at full volume — and it's hard to keep a quiet routine alive while travelling. Back in London I want to keep some of the clarity:

  • Inputs: less phone / laptop; pickier about podcasts, YT, news
  • Practice: sitting + walking meditation; yoga, sports, hot/cold
  • Food: earlier meals, maybe occasional fasts
  • Life: a deep daily project, reading/writing, loving-kindness, community — and re-reading the monastery journal
