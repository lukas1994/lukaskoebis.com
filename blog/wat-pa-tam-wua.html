Wat Pa Tam Wua

Lukas Koebis · September 2026

I spent 8 days at Wat Pa Tam Wua, a Buddhist monastery in northern Thailand (near Mae Hong Son). Apart from a 30-day Sam Harris meditation course through an app that I did 10 years prior, I had no meditation experience. The monastery was amazing — I've never felt as much inner peace and clarity. It felt like my brain went back to its base state after years of phone notifications, emails, podcasts, and YouTube.

After I left I read Jamie Smart's The Little Book of Clarity and felt like I'd just lived his formula: "Clarity = Capacity − Contamination". I'd recommend anyone try this monastery, or a similar one, if you want to see what your mind is like without that contamination.

Monastery life

My brother currently lives in Chiang Mai, so after a few weeks with him we drove five hours along Route 1095 — almost 1000 curves (motion sickness pills recommended!) — to Wat Pa Tam Wua. You don't need to register in advance: check in any day between 6am and 4pm, stay 2 to 10 nights. Some people I met had been there for months. It's free and runs on donations. They give you white clothes, and there's a small shop for mosquito repellent, shower gel, and the like.

The place was founded by Ajahn Luongta Saiyut. He spent decades as an ascetic forest monk meditating in mountain caves and wilderness across Thailand, then set up Wat Pa Tam Wua as a nature-immersed refuge for Thais and foreigners to learn meditation. He didn't give the Dharma talks while I was there, but he made himself available for questions. He seemed like a very happy (and maybe enlightened) guy — he even spoke some German.

Monks follow 277 rules; guests follow the Eight Precepts:

  1. abstinence from taking life
  2. abstinence from taking what is not given
  3. abstinence from unchastity
  4. abstinence from false speech
  5. abstinence from intoxicants which cause a careless frame of mind
  6. abstinence from taking food at the wrong time
  7. abstinence from dancing, music, visiting shows, flowers, make-up, the wearing of ornaments and decorations
  8. abstinence from a luxurious or high sleeping place

Those rules set the tone — especially no food after midday. In practice the monastery is relaxed about guests: people eat at the cafe in the evening, and some used their phones throughout. I turned mine off for the stay, read a lot, took notes, and mostly kept to myself. Strict silent Vipassana retreats ban talking, reading, and writing — that sounds much more intense. Not sure I'd manage it.

It took a few days to get into the rhythm. After two days I almost cut the trip short; I'm glad I stayed. Days 5–7 were the clearest by far.

You wake up early in a surreal setting: the monastery sits between two mountains with a small river running through it — it feels a bit like Avatar.

The day starts with solo meditation, though most people sleep through it and get up for the 6:30 rice offering. Monks aren't allowed to cook, store food overnight, or farm, so the offerings aren't charity so much as a deal: monks provide spiritual guidance and an example of how to live; lay people provide the food — and in giving, practice generosity.

All formal meals are before noon. After the first two days I stuck to that; some people fasted the whole time, which I'd like to try at some point.

I stayed in a shared room on a wooden bed. Sleep was the hardest part — side sleeping on that surface is rough. Single rooms exist; I could have moved and didn't bother.

The 16:00 work session was one of the highlights of the day. You're not supposed to do sports, so physical work feels good after hours of sitting. I started raking leaves, later helped set up a fruit garden, and toward the end ran introduction sessions for new arrivals.

Meditation

Morning and afternoon sessions share the same structure: a short Dharma talk, 60min walking meditation, 40min sitting, 20min lying down. For beginners that's a good setup — two hours of pure sitting wouldn't work for most people.

There are two modes: Samatha (concentration) and Vipassana (insight). Samatha is sharpening the pencil; Vipassana is writing with it — still the mind first, then use that clarity to see how experience actually works.

Walking meditation takes getting used to. You cover about 1km in an hour, barefoot, eyes open, attending to sensation: wet ground, sun on skin, wind on your face. The aim is the "knower" state — the body moving like a robot while you just watch. You can't be knower and thinker at once. One way in is to watch the walking, then watch the mind knowing the walking, until it clicks that the knower knows the movement — not "you". You also can't force the knower to appear. In the last few days I caught it for a few seconds at a time; it needs a lot more practice.

From Walk to be the Knower (monastery site · local copy): "The mind is just the watcher. The body moves and the mind watches. The body ceases to be Ours. This body moves but it is not us moving. True mindfulness arises. The mind awakens and sees that the physical form that is moving is not us."

Sitting and lying-down are Samatha — just the breath. I can't sit on the floor for 40min yet, so I used a chair. I'll work on floor sitting over the next months; every meditation book recommends it. Even on a chair I got to some interesting states.

Evenings were my favourite: chanting in Pali (the Buddha's language), Thai, and English, then another 30min sit. The chanting is melodic, and repeating the same phrases each day means you sink into it after a few nights. You start meditating just before dark; when you open your eyes again it's night. Sometimes it rained. The vibes were 10/10 — and over six hours after the last meal, the mind is clear.

On my last two nights I think I hit an absorption like what people call a Jhana state — I'm not sure enough of the term to claim the real thing. Each breath in felt like bright light through the body. You're grateful for the breath; just existing feels joyful. Each episode lasted maybe 5–10 minutes. You stop forcing focus on the breath, keep a little control of attention, and can still have thoughts (e.g. steer them toward visuals).

What worked for me technically: take 5–10 deep breaths and follow the air with your mind as it moves in through the nose and down into the body. Then stop controlling and watch the natural breath at one point on the nose. In and out usually hit slightly different spots for me; I tried to find one point both pass through. When thoughts appear, notice them and return to the breath. At home that happens constantly. A few days in at the monastery I almost never got pulled away. After 5–10 minutes of steady focus you drop into a deeper state where you don't have to chase the breath anymore — you just stay. From there I tried holding faint visuals in the dark; that was hard, but that's how I reached the absorption twice during lie-down.

Another sitting experience: the body felt like it was deforming. Hands on legs, then the distance between head and legs seemed to stretch and shrink, then body and mind collapsed to a single point. That was scary — I opened my eyes and stopped. I haven't gotten back there. It might be closer to no-self (below).

One monk put it simply: we spend hours on the body — eating, brushing teeth, the gym — and almost no time on the mind. Meditation is gym for the mind. A few minutes morning or evening is already worth it.

Buddhism

This was a Theravada monastery (mainly Southeast Asia; Mahayana is more Northeast Asia). What I know here comes from a one-day course in Chiang Mai plus reading on site — so limited. Happy to be corrected.

I treated Buddhism as a philosophy and practice rather than a religion, though in Thailand (and elsewhere) it clearly functions as one: temples, ritual, the full stack.

The Buddha lived around the 5th–6th century BC. The core framing is the Four Noble Truths:

  • Dukkha: existence is bound up with suffering
  • Samudaya: the cause is desire
  • Nirodha: suffering can end
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path is the way out

The Noble Eightfold Path:

  • Right view, thought (Wisdom, Paññā)
  • Right speech, action, livelihood (Morality, Sīla)
  • Right effort, mindfulness, concentration (Concentration, Samādhi)

Wisdom here means contemplating the three Characteristics of Existence: impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anattā).

There's ordinary wisdom — words, concepts — and a deeper knowing that only shows up in experience.

From Hesse's Siddhartha: "Wisdom cannot be passed on. Wisdom which a wise man tries to pass on to someone always sounds like foolishness."

"Knowledge can be conveyed, but not wisdom. It can be found, it can be lived […] but it cannot be expressed in words and taught."

I asked a bunch of questions after Dharma talks and to the Abbot. The answers were never very concrete: more practice, more years; your body and mind are the teacher. Annoying if you want explanations — and also the point. The teaching monk kept returning to a story along these lines: a student is asked whether he believes the Buddha, says no, and the Buddha says good — don't believe me, investigate yourself. I couldn't find that exact Q&A in the suttas; the closest official version is the Kalama Sutta, where the Buddha tells people not to take teachings on authority (including "the monk is our teacher") and to know for themselves. That was the vibe of every answer I got.

Four Foundations of Mindfulness (Satipatthana)

  • mindfulness of the body
  • mindfulness of feelings
  • mindfulness of the mind
  • mindfulness of mental qualities (dhamma)

Most people start with the body (the breath). That practice predates the Buddha. He spent years on it and broke through when he turned to mindfulness of the mind. The monk who taught us said the same about his own practice — and that more logical people often progress faster that way.

Takeaways

The mind is the only source of truth we actually have. Physics and "reality" still arrive through it. Spending serious time looking at how your mind works seems under-rated.

What I like most in the Buddhist framing is that your own body and mind are the teacher, and nobody can walk the path for you. That should land with a lot of technical / rational people, and I wouldn't be surprised if it keeps growing in the West.

I still can't get behind rebirth, kamma, and parts of the broader world-view in the Dhamma. And if I tried to live a full Buddhist life, I think I'd miss procreation, innovation, music, beauty, and art.

I've been reading more of the scientific side since. Why Buddhism Is True by Robert Wright is the one I'm in now: evolutionary psychology meeting meditation. The bit that clicked is that the "self" isn't a CEO — more like competing modules (craving, status, fear) with a stitched-together feeling of "I" after the fact. Mindfulness lets you see a craving module fire without obeying it. That maps cleanly onto knower vs thinker and no-self, just in different words. Related: Anil Seth's Being You, Goleman and Davidson's Altered Traits, and Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?".

Stoicism vs Buddhism

  • Emotions: both say don't react on impulse. A Stoic revises the judgment ("this isn't inherently bad; my take makes it so"). A Buddhist watches the feeling without becoming it and lets it pass.
  • Impermanence: Stoics use Memento Mori to stay present and harden against loss. Buddhists sit with anicca until attachment to temporary things loosens.

From the monastery I flew to Shanghai with friends. China impressed me (another post) and also sat at the opposite pole — a £30 bath house open 24h with unlimited food and games, consumerism at full volume — which made a quiet routine hard to keep. Back in London I want to hold onto some of the clarity:

  • Inputs: less phone / laptop; pickier about podcasts, YouTube, and news
  • Practice: sitting and walking meditation; yoga, sports, hot/cold; fewer sensual defaults
  • Food: earlier meals, maybe occasional fasts
  • Life: a deep daily project, reading and writing, loving-kindness, community — and re-reading the monastery journal
